<section id="foundation">

## The platform, not another gallery

Greenfly is how sports and entertainment organizations capture, collect, organize, and send short-form media. Every season, campaign, and show runs through that library. When the library cannot keep up, neither can the rest of the product.

Media Library 2.0 started because the current gallery had become the ceiling. Customers were working around it. Future work — search, metadata, automation, permissions, ingestion, AI — needed a foundation that did not exist yet.

I ran discovery, design, and the first stretch of implementation with product, engineering, customer success, and customers. Engineering is still shipping. That is part of the story, not a reason to wait.

This was not another page in Media Library. We were deciding how every customer would work with content for years.

</section>

<section id="challenge">

## The library had become the limiting factor

The gallery was flat. Customers already had a structure. Greenfly did not.

The workarounds were specific. A Nuggets producer keeps an Excel sheet of gallery names and tags because uploaders do not name things the same way. The NBA is sitting on about 165,000 assets and more than 2,000 galleries, with no hierarchy, next to a SharePoint structure they already use. The NFL is closer to 300,000 assets. Time filters block a historical search, and the description is “the most important piece of info.” The grid hides it, so they live in the table. Premier League Productions called the archive a “smorgasbord of old stuff.”

The problem was not that people wanted folders. The library could not hold the way they already ran a season, a campaign, or a show.

<section id="discovery">

## Discovery at a scale the old library never had

I treated this as product strategy work, not a UI audit. If we were going to rebuild the foundation, it had to hold up across more than one team and one vertical.

It was two rounds with the same customers coming back. Discovery ran September through October 2025. Wireframes went back to them in November. Search tests ran January and February 2026. Pro sports (MLB live content and player social, the NBA, the NFL, the Revolution, Premier League Productions), college (Illinois, Iowa, Wisconsin), and entertainment (Bravo, Peloton, Universal, MSNBC and MS NOW). Product and customer success were in the room. They already carried the workarounds.

Bringing the same people back to the wires mattered more than adding a new interview. They were shaping the model, not reacting to a concept once.

</section>

<section id="patterns">

## Every customer organized differently. The model was the same.

If you listen interview by interview, it sounds like disagreement.

Sports talks in seasons and games. College talks in campaigns and tournaments. Entertainment talks in productions, movies, and shows. Labels never matched. Spreadsheets never matched.

Underneath, the work was the same.

People grouped content into a hierarchy that followed the business: a container for the year or property, then the campaign or production, then the work inside it. They collected assets into that structure, found them again later, and sent them out.

Different words. Same jobs. Same nesting.

That became a decision I tested, not a metaphor. Up to three folder levels. The gallery is the fourth. Assets live in galleries, not in folders. Bravo rated that model a 4 and understood assets do not live in folders. Peloton said four levels is enough and they would likely use three: program, year, discipline. The Nuggets mapped it to season, then opponent, then game. Universal wanted folders, then galleries, then assets, so talent would not face a flat list of 20 galleries.

One model. Their labels.

</section>

<section id="strategy">

## Three jobs, not a feature list

Once the pattern was clear, the strategy got simpler. Every decision had to support three jobs:

**Collect.** Get content in without losing where it belongs. For a lot of teams that is an import, not an upload. Colleges watch a PhotoShelter, Box, or Drive folder. Iowa’s confidence in the homepage dropped until those imports were on it.  
**Organize.** Let the hierarchy match the business. Archive is part of that job. Bravo and MS NOW archive a season or a show. MSNBC archives bookings the day of, films after about two weeks, podcasts weekly, and called archive “incredibly useful.”  
**Distribute.** Find it again and send it out. Saved search is for the team that runs the same query every week. Bravo browses by show and does not expect to save a search. MS NOW called saved searches extremely useful for re-air copy. Both can be true.

Those jobs became the product principles. If a request did not make collect, organize, or distribute clearer, it waited. Carousel posting, gallery duplication, public links, and SharePoint sync came up. They sit behind hierarchy, import, and search.

That framing also made the conversation with engineering and leadership easier. We were arguing for a content operations platform the rest of the roadmap could sit on — search, metadata, automation, AI, permissions, ingestion.

</section>

<section id="system">

## Designing around the problems, not a screen tour

Design started after the model was shared, not before. I did not walk the team through a set of mockups and hope the IA showed up. We designed against the jobs.

The system had to answer a short list of hard problems:

**Hierarchy.** Up to three folder levels, then the gallery. A season, a campaign, a production, or a show can use that shape without learning a new one.

**Home.** Land in the work in front of you. Quick actions were a win for Peloton, Bravo, Iowa, and Wisconsin. The NFL said they would not change the day. They go to the library and search by description. The actions also have to change by role. MLB live-content producers need upload. They should not be able to delete a gallery full of content, and invite has to be tiered the same way.

**Search.** Find across the hierarchy, then keep the query if the team will run it again. I did not leave the pattern to a vote. The test compared two approaches on time to the right asset, wrong turns, and trust. Product and design make the call for the 80 percent case. Customers also disagreed on the logic. Illinois wanted AND, as in LeBron and Nike. The Nuggets wanted OR, so a miss in the tags does not hide the asset. AI tags split the same way. The NFL leans on them because human tags are inconsistent. Illinois wants them off, because duplicate jersey numbers tag the wrong player.

**Moving assets, bulk actions, and labels.** Operations teams re-home content and move more than one file. Verticals share the model and still need their own names.

<section id="delivery">

## Validation with customers, then tradeoffs with engineering

We took the model back to the people who had described it. The November wires changed what we kept. Some hierarchy ideas were too rigid. Some search ideas were too early. The jobs stayed.

The product is in alpha now. Beta is the middle of October. General availability is before the end of the year. What we are validating is the model itself, especially how people organize content. Follow-ups with key customers are scheduled. They are not done. This case study stays open as those land.

Implementation started before every question was closed. I worked with product and engineering on what had to be real first: a structure you can create, assets you can place, a home that matches the work, and permissions that change with the role. Migration off the old gallery, who can delete, and how content gets imported are product decisions. I stayed in them.

Phasing mattered. Search, automation, and AI on a library that still thought in a flat gallery would have rebuilt the ceiling. The first release is the model. The next releases are what the model unlocks.

</section>

<section id="impact">

## The outcome so far is the platform, not a launch metric

Engineering is still in it. I am not going to invent a usage number, and I am not going to treat an alpha as proof.

What we do have is an early signal and a dated path. In the November wireframe reviews, several teams rated homepage relevance, and their confidence they could start their top tasks, around 4 out of 5. The four-level model was understood without a new mental model. Iowa’s confidence dropped until imports were on the homepage, which is why collect is an import path and not only an upload button. Customers are now in alpha against that organization model. Beta is the middle of October. General availability is before the end of the year. The follow-ups that test how people actually organize are still ahead of us.

The other outcome is the roadmap. Media Library is the foundation the next work sits on: search, metadata, automation, permissions, and AI. That sequence came out of the research. Structure and a role-aware home first. Search on top of a library that can hold the work.

</section>

<section id="learned">

## I thought folders were the problem

I went into this project believing we needed a better way to file assets.

I left realizing organization was not the problem. Mental models were. Every customer already had one. Greenfly simply was not reflecting it.

The biggest insight was not hierarchy. It was that the hierarchy already existed in how people talked about their season, their campaign, their show. Our job was to stop asking them to translate.

**What’s next.** Finish the alpha validation, including the follow-ups on how people organize, then beta and general availability. Search, automation, and recommendations come after a library that can hold the work. AI only helps if the foundation is honest.

</section>
