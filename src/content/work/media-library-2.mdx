<section id="foundation">

## The platform, not another gallery

Greenfly is how sports and entertainment organizations capture, collect, organize, and send short-form media. Every season, campaign, and show runs through that library. When the library cannot keep up, neither can the rest of the product.

Media Library 2.0 started because the current gallery had become the ceiling. Customers were working around it. Future work — search, metadata, automation, permissions, ingestion, AI — needed a foundation that did not exist yet.

I ran discovery, design, and the first stretch of implementation with product, engineering, customer success, and customers. Engineering is still shipping. That is part of the story, not a reason to wait.

This was not another page in Media Library. We were deciding how every customer would work with content for years.

</section>

<section id="challenge">

## The library had become the limiting factor

The gallery was flat and fragmented. Customers could not organize the way they already thought about their work. Finding an asset meant scrolling, guessing, or leaving Greenfly.

I kept hearing the same workarounds:

- Spreadsheets next to the product, because the library could not hold the structure
- One-off folders and naming schemes that only one person understood
- Painful onboarding every time a season or campaign started over
- No clean way to scale the same operation across seasons, teams, or properties

The problem was not that people wanted “folders.” The problem was that Greenfly did not reflect how they already ran content operations.

Greenfly’s current gallery structure is flat and fragmented. It fights the way customers think about seasons, campaigns, productions, and shows — and it blocks the platform from growing.

**How might we rebuild Media Library so it matches the way customers already organize work, and so the rest of Greenfly can build on it?**

</section>

<section id="discovery">

## Discovery at a scale the old library never had

I treated this as product strategy work, not a UI audit. If we were going to rebuild the foundation, we needed to hear it from more than one team and one vertical.

Discovery included:

- 25+ customer interviews
- Three verticals: professional sports, college, and entertainment
- Jobs-to-be-done
- Competitive analysis
- Customer workshops and co-design sessions
- Synthesis with PM and customer success
- Alignment with leadership before we committed the roadmap

PM and CS were in it from the start. They already carried the workarounds. I needed their partnership — and executive buy-in — because this was going to change how the company talked about the product, not just how a gallery looked.

Customers asked for more research time after the first rounds. They felt heard and wanted to help shape it. That is unusual. It also told us we were on a problem they actually lived with.

</section>

<section id="patterns">

## Every customer organized differently. The model was the same.

If you listen interview by interview, it sounds like disagreement.

Sports talks in seasons and games. College talks in campaigns and tournaments. Entertainment talks in productions, movies, and shows. Labels never matched. Spreadsheets never matched.

Underneath, the work was the same.

People grouped content into a hierarchy that followed the business: a container for the year or property, then the campaign or production, then the work inside it. They collected assets into that structure, found them again later, and sent them out.

Different words. Same jobs. Same nesting.

That was the insight that changed the project. We were not designing a better flat gallery. We were designing for a mental model customers already had — one Greenfly had never shown them.

</section>

<section id="strategy">

## Three jobs, not a feature list

Once the pattern was clear, the strategy got simpler. Every decision had to support three jobs:

**Collect.** Get content in without losing where it belongs.  
**Organize.** Let the hierarchy match the business, not a generic folder tree.  
**Distribute.** Find it again and send it out without leaving the system.

Those jobs became the product principles. If a request did not make collect, organize, or distribute clearer, it waited.

That framing also made the conversation with engineering and leadership easier. We were not arguing for folders. We were arguing for a content operations platform the rest of the roadmap could sit on — search, metadata, automation, AI, permissions, ingestion.

</section>

<section id="system">

## Designing around the problems, not a screen tour

Design started after the model was shared, not before. I did not walk the team through a set of mockups and hope the IA showed up. We designed against the jobs.

The system had to answer a short list of hard problems:

**Hierarchy.** Create and live in a structure that can be a season, a campaign, a production, or a show — without teaching a new mental model.

**Home and navigation.** Land in the work you are in, not a flat sea of assets.

**Moving assets.** Re-home content when the real world changes, which it always does.

**Search and saved searches.** Find across the hierarchy, then keep the queries people run every week.

**Bulk actions.** Operations teams do not move one file at a time.

**Customization.** Verticals share a model and still need their own labels and defaults.

These are the wireframes from the customer-call prototype. They are the screens we put in front of people while the model was still being tested, not a launch build.

Home has to land in the work, and an empty library has to offer a way in — including the sources colleges already collect from.

<section id="delivery">

## Validation with customers, then tradeoffs with engineering

We took the model back to the people who had described it. Co-design and reviews changed what we kept. Some hierarchy ideas were too rigid. Some search ideas were too early. The jobs stayed. The UI moved.

Implementation started before every question was closed. That is the honest version. I worked with PM and engineering on what had to ship first so the foundation was real: structure you can create, assets you can place, a home that matches the work.

Phasing mattered. If we tried to land search, automation, and AI on a library that still thought in a flat gallery, we would have rebuilt the ceiling. The first release is the model. The next releases are what the model unlocks.

Technical constraints showed up immediately — migration from the old gallery, permissions, ingestion paths. Those are product decisions. I stayed in them instead of handing over a file and stepping back.

</section>

<section id="impact">

## The outcome so far is the platform, not a launch metric

Engineering is still in it. I am not going to invent a usage number.

What we do have:

- A shared product strategy across design, PM, CS, and leadership
- A roadmap that treats Media Library as the foundation, not a feature
- Customers who asked to keep going because they recognized their own model
- CS feedback that the direction matches the workarounds they have been supporting for years
- A clear place for search, metadata, automation, and AI to land later

That is the impact of this phase. We changed what Greenfly is building next, not only what a gallery looks like.

</section>

<section id="learned">

## I thought folders were the problem

I went into this project believing we needed a better way to file assets.

I left realizing organization was not the problem. Mental models were. Every customer already had one. Greenfly simply was not reflecting it.

The biggest insight was not hierarchy. It was that the hierarchy already existed in how people talked about their season, their campaign, their show. Our job was to stop asking them to translate.

**What’s next.** Finish the implementation, then put search, automation, and recommendations on top of a library that can actually hold the work. AI only helps if the foundation is honest.

</section>
